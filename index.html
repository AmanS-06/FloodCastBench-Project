Can a neural network forecast a flood?

A CNN-LSTM project on four simulated floods: Australia, the UK, Pakistan and Mozambique. Deep Learning CA3, Anhad Mahajan and Aman Sumeet Saxena.

Scroll down. The picture on the right changes as you read.

Part 1: The floods

A flood, 30 minutes at a time

We worked with FloodCastBench, a public dataset of four real floods that were re-created in a computer simulation. Each flood is a long series of water-depth maps, one every 30 minutes. Our goal was to look at the last few maps and guess the next one.

Australia, February 2022

Every map is a grid of squares. For each square we know the ground height, what covers the ground (crops, trees, buildings, water) and how deep the water is. Australia is the biggest map, over a million squares of 30 m each.

UK, December 2015

The UK flood is much smaller and only lasts three days. We never trained on it. It was kept aside as a flood the model had never seen.

Pakistan, August 2022

Pakistan is huge, so its squares are 480 m wide instead of 30 m. Water moves across a 480 m square very differently, so it got its own model.

Mozambique, March 2019

Mozambique (Cyclone Idai) is the second never-seen flood. The 480 m model learned on Pakistan and was tested here.

Rain drives the flood

Over the ten days, the rain comes in bursts and the water keeps building up. Most of the flooding happens after the heavy rain in the second half.

Which rain belongs to which map?

Before training anything we checked when each rain file falls. Each file holds the rain of the 30 minutes after its map. This mattered: the model is only allowed to see rain that has already fallen, never future rain.

Do the layers line up?

The rain and ground files come on a finer grid than some depth maps. We moved them around by a few squares and checked where rivers and valleys matched the water best. A shifted layer would quietly teach the model wrong things.

Everything on one grid

After this step every layer describes exactly the same place: water depth, ground height, land cover and rain.

Water in = rain in

Over a whole map, the water rises by almost exactly the amount of rain that falls. The totals differ by at most about 5%, because a little water leaves at the edge of the map. We used this later as a physics check on our forecasts.

Part 2: The model

CNN + ConvLSTM

The CNN layers look at each of the last four maps and pick out shapes: water edges, slopes, patches of rain. The ConvLSTM then reads the four maps in order and remembers how the water has been moving. The model predicts only the change in depth for the next 30 minutes.

How we score a forecast

Over 30 minutes most of the map hardly changes, so just copying the last map is already a very good forecast. We score everything against that. A skill of 0 means no better than copying and 1 means perfect.

The whole system

Around the forecaster we built the parts the course asked for: a capsule network that rates how bad the flood will be, written rules that check its answer, a water-balance rule that checks the forecast, and Grad-CAM to show where the model looks.

Part 3: What happened

Our first model beat copying...

Model A beat copying the last map on three of the four tests, including both floods it had never seen. On the last part of the Australia flood it did worse than copying.

...but only by a little

At the busiest moments of the UK and Mozambique floods, Model A's error is clearly below copying. At calm moments the lines meet.

What the forecasts look like

The predicted change follows the true change in the right places, along the UK river and the rain blocks in Pakistan, but it is weaker than the real thing.

A one-number formula did far better

Then we tried a trend forecast: take the last map and keep the last 30-minute change going, slightly damped. It has no network at all and scored 0.91 to 0.99. Our second model, Model B, starts from this trend and learns a small correction. It matched the trend but did not beat it.

Trend and Model B overlap

Over the whole test period the trend forecast and Model B sit almost on top of each other, far below copying and Model A.

Where the mistakes are (30 m)

Model A's errors show up along the rivers. Model B's error map is almost blank at the same colour scale.

Where the mistakes are (480 m)

Same story for Pakistan and Mozambique.

Not much left to improve

After the trend forecast, only 1% to 9% of the error of copying is left. That is why Model B can't add much at 30 minutes. A fair test of the LSTM's memory needs longer forecasts, 6 to 24 hours ahead.

Part 4: A bigger study on a laptop

Simple models get most of the way

In a larger study of about 310 training runs, forecasting up to 2.5 hours ahead, a simple lookup table that only uses each squarea simple linear model of each square's recent depths already scored 0.79 to 0.82#x27;s current depth and last change already scored 0.74 to 0.79. The best networks reached 0.81 to 0.87.

Does memory help?

When both were trained for the same length, the ConvLSTM and a plain CNN scored the same. The ConvLSTM did lose skill more slowly as the forecast reached further ahead.

The models live on the recent depths

Freeze the depth history and the skill drops to about zero. Remove the rain or the ground height and it barely moves.

What tuning changed

A bigger batch with a higher learning rate made things slightly worse. Training longer helped a little. Anything under about 0.02 is within the noise from the random seed.

Six predictions, written down first

Before the final runs we wrote down six predictions. Some held, some only partly, and one mostly failed.

Where models break

When we hid a block of time from training, the plain CNN failed badly on Pakistan. Almost all its error came from six windows at the very start of the flood, which it had never seen. The ConvLSTM and the lookup table coped.

Part 5: How bad, and why

Fixing the severity label

The first severity label used the deepest single square in a tile, so almost every tile came out 'severe'. We replaced it with the share of normally dry land under more than 30 cm of water.

The capsule network

A capsule network rates each tile as None, Minor, Moderate or Severe for 30 minutes ahead. It got 50% to 87% right. That sounds fine until you notice the rating in 30 minutes is almost the same as the rating now.

Rules that check the network

Three written rules check the capsule network and give a reason for every decision. They raised the accuracy in all four floods. Reading the rating straight from the forecast map was right about 99% of the time.

Where the forecaster looks

Grad-CAM colours the parts of the input that mattered most. For the forecaster the bright areas follow river channels, flood edges and rain blocks, the places where the water is actually changing.

Where the capsule network looks

For the capsule network the heat maps were weak and changed between training runs, so we treat them as pictures, not proof.

What we learned

  • At 30 minutes ahead, a simple trend forecast does almost all the work. Our second model matches it but doesn't beat it.
  • Up to 2.5 hours ahead, a simple model of each square's recent depths gets most of the way. The LSTM's memory didn't help at equal training.
  • The biggest lesson: always compare a model with simple forecasts. Our first model looked good until we did.
  • Next we want to forecast 6 to 24 hours ahead, where a trend should break down and memory could finally matter.
